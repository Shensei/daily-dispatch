Daily Technical Snippet

Cache-Augmented Generation (CAG) — when the knowledge fits in the context window, skip retrieval and just preload it

RAG fetches chunks at query time; CAG does the opposite. If your knowledge base is small and stable enough to fit in a long context window, you load the whole thing in once, precompute the KV-cache, and answer every query against that cached state — no retriever, no vector DB, no per-query search step. The payoff is real: you delete retrieval latency and the whole class of “the retriever grabbed the wrong chunk” errors, and the model reasons over the full corpus at once instead of a top-k slice. The catch is just as real — you're bounded by context length and token cost, the cache goes stale the moment the corpus changes, and everything fuses into one opaque blob with no per-fact provenance.

Learn more

That last catch is exactly the Kepler seam. CAG trades away the retriever — and with it the audit trail: once everything is welded into one KV-cache you can't say which source produced an answer, or revoke or update a single fact without rebuilding the whole thing. So CAG and owned-memory aren't rivals, they're layers: preload the stable, public corpus for speed, and keep the user-specific, changing, must-be-auditable facts in a retrieval layer where every recall is attributable. The Kepler read: the facts you'd never dump into a shared cache — personal, mutable, provenance-critical — are precisely the ones worth addressing by URL and owning.

→ Chan et al., “Don't Do RAG: When Cache-Augmented Generation is All You Need for Knowledge Tasks”
